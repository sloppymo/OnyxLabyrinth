# Onyx Labyrinth — Death and Birth Worldbuilding Bible

**Date:** 2026-08-20  
**Status:** Current narrative direction. Dialogue infrastructure and the first
Great Gate portrait art are implemented; broader campaign placement remains in
progress.  
**Supersedes:** the fixed “bring the gods back” ending and any claim that the
lamp can only restore Death in
`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-07-25-labyrinth-narrative-design.md`. The existing
century-wipe mechanics, Edgehollow, the kept, the three named bosses, the lamp,
and the one-wish rule remain useful canon unless this document conflicts with
them.

## 1. The premise

The world has gone too long without two things: **Death** and **Birth**.

People can fall, starve, burn, drown, and disappear into a century of darkness,
but they do not end. Eventually they wake again. No human child has been born
for longer than anyone can meaningfully remember. The same exhausted people
keep returning to the same exhausted world.

The gods left one exception buried at the center of the labyrinth: a lamp whose
inhabitant can grant one final wish. It is not a trick and the wish is not
corrupted by malicious wording. The horror is that one honest wish cannot mend
everything.

Two claimants understand the choice more clearly than anyone else:

- **The Old Man** wants the lamp to restore Death. With no Birth to replace the
  people who finally die, humanity will diminish and eventually cease to exist.
  He regards that ending as mercy.
- **The Rat King** wants the lamp to restore Birth. Humanity can multiply,
  rebuild farms and cities, and perhaps rediscover purpose. Rats will multiply
  with them and thrive in everything humans build. He regards that future as
  abundance.

Neither offers a complete world. Death without Birth ends humanity. Birth
without Death creates growth with no release. The lamp has one wish.

## 2. What “deathless” means

Deathlessness is not invulnerability.

- Bodies fail. Pain, hunger, disease, dismemberment, drowning, and combat still
  matter.
- A total party defeat is a long interval of darkness, followed by waking
  again. The century counter is a literal part of the setting.
- Nobody ages into a grave. A person may look weathered, starved, or ancient,
  but time alone cannot finish them.
- Living systems persist but do not renew. Old groves, root stocks, livestock,
  and vermin remain; no genuinely new life enters the world.
- Buildings, roads, tools, and institutions are not alive. They decay normally.
  This is why the world can become emptier even while its people cannot leave
  it.

Agriculture is maintenance of dwindling remnants, not a cycle of sowing and
harvest. A settlement may keep an ancient orchard producing or nurse the last
living field, but most people have stopped doing work whose future they will
never live to see. Cities failed because their citizens lost the habits and
hope that continually rebuild a city—not because all their residents died.

The result is not a crowded immortal paradise. It is a scattered population of
wanderers repeatedly breaking down and returning, while the structures that
once gave their lives shape continue to disappear.

## 3. The labyrinth and the lamp

The labyrinth is both a physical dungeon and a lock around the last act of
creation left in the world. Its floors descend while curling inward; **the
center** is the lamp chamber reached at the end of that descent. “The center”
and “the bottom” may both describe that final chamber, but player-facing copy
should prefer **the center** from this point forward.

The prison’s hazards are deliberate. Darkness, antimagic, traps, wardens, and
the kept are the world’s argument against anyone reaching the lamp.

The lamp’s rules are simple:

1. It contains one wish.
2. It can restore a missing law of the world, including Birth or Death.
3. It cannot divide one wish into two wishes.
4. It does not twist intent or punish imprecise grammar.
5. Once the wish is made, the lamp goes dark.

The djinn is a witness, not a moral judge. It will not tell the party which
future is correct.

## 4. The Old Man

### Public case

The Old Man says that an ending is a right. Every defeat currently becomes a
pause; every grief remains present; every obligation can be postponed forever
and therefore loses its meaning. Restoring Death makes choices matter because a
life can finally be completed.

### Private truth

He does not merely want the option to die for himself. He expects humanity to
end. With Birth still absent, every final death reduces the human population
forever. He considers extinction gentler than another age of compulsory
existence.

This is not a concealed villain reveal. He should state the consequence plainly
once the party is deep enough to understand it. His danger is certainty: he has
lived so long inside despair that he cannot believe future people might want a
future.

### Voice

- Short sentences, little ornament.
- Concrete words: *stop, rest, enough, quiet, finish*.
- Never cackles, threatens humanity, or calls himself merciful.
- Does not deny the Rat King’s factual case; he denies that more life is an
  answer.
- His rare tenderness should make his position harder, not easier, to dismiss.

## 5. The Rat King

### Public case

The Rat King says purpose requires a future. Birth would make teaching,
building, farming, and protecting something more than maintenance. A child
would be a person for whom the world is new rather than a prison already
memorized.

### Private truth

He primarily wants **more humans**. Rats flourish in the ecological wake of
human abundance: granaries, mills, cellars, sewers, rubbish, warm walls, and
stored food. The scattered deathless wanderers cannot sustain a great rat
kingdom. Restored Birth would give rats litters again and humans generations
again, letting both populations expand without Death to limit them.

He does not hide this when directly accused. He likes humans, but his affection
is not disinterested. He would prefer humans to be happy because happy humans
build, farm, gather, celebrate, and leave crumbs. If happiness and abundance
diverge, abundance is his first loyalty.

His sincere dream is to see a human child. It is the smallest, cleanest image
of the future he wants and the part of his argument that is not reducible to
hunger.

### Voice

- Quick, specific, sensuous language: *grain, brick, smoke, kitchens, streets,
  crumbs, warm walls*.
- Amused by accusations he considers obviously true.
- Says “more” more often than “better.”
- Can be funny, but never harmless or merely comic relief.
- His honesty about selfishness makes him more persuasive, not less dangerous.

## 6. Their relationship

The Old Man and the Rat King are recurring rivals, not a hero and villain pair.
They have argued across many descents and already know the shape of each other’s
case. Their scenes should feel like an old dispute resumed in front of new
witnesses.

The Old Man sees the Rat King as appetite pretending to be hope. The Rat King
sees the Old Man as exhaustion pretending to be wisdom. Both descriptions are
partly true.

They should sometimes speak to one another, sometimes approach the party alone,
and sometimes be overheard talking with an existing labyrinth NPC. This keeps
the argument embodied. A starving trader, a kept guardian, or somebody tending
an ancient garden can challenge either position more effectively than another
abstract speech.

They are not required to travel with the party continuously. The labyrinth can
separate and reunite paths in ways that feel impossible; recurring encounters
should make both figures seem to be pursuing the center on routes of their own.

## 7. Human and nonhuman perspectives

NPC positions should grow from what each person does every day:

- **Edgehollow maintainers** may favor Birth because apprentices and successors
  would make their work meaningful again.
- **The repeatedly wounded** may favor Death without speaking in cosmic terms.
  They want the next injury to be the last.
- **Farm and garden keepers** understand the Rat King’s case but can name its
  cost: more mouths in a world where nothing can yet die.
- **The kept** are not philosophical authorities. Their condition is evidence
  of endlessness, not a tidy endorsement of either wish.
- **Rats and other remnants** also lack new generations. The Rat King is
  arguing for his species as surely as for humanity.
- **Party members** should use class/personality combat barks for immediate
  reactions, while their considered position belongs in labyrinth dialogue.

No NPC should exist only to announce which ending is morally correct.

## 8. Story spine

Exact coordinates and final copy remain a content-placement pass. The intended
reveal order is:

1. **Surface / Floor 1 — the right to stop.** The Old Man names Death as his
   goal, but not humanity’s extinction.
2. **Floor 1 or 2 — the promise of more.** The Rat King introduces Birth through
   farms, kitchens, and cities rather than metaphysics.
3. **Floor 2 — lived testimony.** Each speaks with a labyrinth NPC whose daily
   existence complicates the claim.
4. **Floor 3 — the first joint argument.** The party sees that only one missing
   law can be restored.
5. **Floor 4 — honest motives.** The Rat King admits that human abundance feeds
   rat abundance. The Old Man admits that Death without Birth will end
   humanity.
6. **Floor 5 — the child.** The Rat King’s wish to see a human child lands after
   his selfishness is already known. The Old Man asks what kind of world that
   child would inherit.
7. **The center — one wish.** The lamp does not editorialize. The decision and
   its consequences belong to the party/player.

The current shipped fixed ending still restores the gods and Death. Replacing
that screen with the final Birth/Death decision is a separate implementation
pass; the dialogue system in this change does not silently alter campaign
completion logic.

## 9. Dialogue presentation rules

### Labyrinth events

Full conversations are modal `UiStack` overlays over the live corridor. Each
line has one speaker, and the panel alternates portrait side with the speaker.
The line may paginate, and a node may end in player choices that branch to
other nodes. The underlying `GameState.mode` remains `"dungeon"`.

Floor events retain the existing one-time/repeatable persistence contract. An
event references a conversation by stable id and keeps a short fallback:

```ts
{
  x: 12,
  y: 7,
  kind: "message",
  message: "Two figures are arguing in the dark.",
  dialogueId: "rat-king-old-man-thesis",
  once: true,
}
```

The authored graph lives in `src/data/dialogue-events.ts`. Gameplay effects do
not live inside dialogue data; a future caller may react to stable choice ids.

### Combat

Combat never opens the full dialogue panel. Speech is a `CombatEvent` bark
painted above the speaking actor by the shared choreography and both render
backends.

- One short sentence, ideally under 34 characters.
- No choices, pagination, lore paragraphs, or blocking input.
- Attach to a semantic combat landmark (anticipation, release, contact,
  reaction, or settle), not an arbitrary wall-clock timeout.
- Use the combat actor instance id so the bark follows the live sprite.
- A scene that needs more than two exchanged lines belongs before or after the
  fight as a labyrinth dialogue event.

## 10. Portrait art contract

Rat King and Old Man dialogue portraits ship at the stable ids (`rat-king`,
`old-man`). They are approved 16-bit head-and-shoulders portraits packaged and
exported through Aseprite. The presenter still renders a compact initial card
for any future speaker whose art has not been approved, rather than a broken
image or an enlarged combat sprite.

To ship a portrait later:

1. Add the cleaned portrait at
   `public/assets/portraits/<portrait-id>/portrait.png`.
2. Add the id to `NPC_PORTRAIT_IDS` in `src/engine/npc-portraits.ts`.
3. Use a vertical head-and-shoulders composition with no baked UI border; the
   dialogue panel supplies its own frame.
4. Verify left/right speakers, narrow layout, and the missing-art fallback.

The supplied animated Rat King and Old Man GIFs remain character/combat
references for the portraits. They are not shipped as dialogue portraits.

## 11. Canon guardrails

- Do not describe the Rat King as altruistic. He likes humans and wants to use
  them; both are true.
- Do not describe the Old Man as seeking immediate mass murder. He wants Death
  restored and accepts the eventual extinction that follows without Birth.
- Do not let either plan restore both Birth and Death. The one-wish constraint
  is the conflict.
- Do not make the djinn a trickster or add a wording loophole.
- Do not use a human child merely as sentiment before the Rat King’s appetite
  has been established.
- Do not put full conversations in combat UI.
- Do not ship the character GIFs as portrait substitutes.
